# 1. What is Reading Comprehension?

Reading comprehension is the ability to understand a passage, identify its important information, interpret the writer's meaning, and answer questions using evidence from the text.

PET comprehension questions may test:

- Main idea
- Specific facts/details
- Inference/conclusion
- Word meaning in context
- Suitable title
- Reference of a word/phrase
- Cause and effect
- Sequence/order
- Writer's purpose or tone

::: tip
**Golden Rule:** The passage is the primary source of evidence. Do not select an answer simply because it is generally true or because you already know something about the topic.
:::

# 2. Types of Reading Questions

| Question Type | What it asks |
|---|---|
| Main Idea | What is the passage mainly about? |
| Detail/Fact | What does the passage explicitly state? |
| Inference | What can reasonably be concluded? |
| Vocabulary | What does a word mean in context? |
| Title | Which title best represents the whole passage? |
| Reference | What does a pronoun/phrase refer to? |
| Cause/Effect | Why did something happen / what resulted? |
| Sequence | What happened first/next/last? |
| Purpose | Why did the writer include this information? |
| Tone | What attitude does the writer convey? |

# 3. How to Read a Passage

## Step 1 — Read the title, if given

The title often provides the broad subject.

## Step 2 — Read the first sentence carefully

The opening often introduces the topic or context.

## Step 3 — Notice paragraph structure

Look for:

- topic sentence
- supporting details
- examples
- contrast
- cause/effect
- conclusion

## Step 4 — Mark logical connectors mentally

Important connectors include:

**however, therefore, because, although, moreover, consequently, for example, finally**.

## Step 5 — Answer from evidence

Return to the relevant sentence or paragraph whenever possible.

::: strategy
**Read → Understand → Locate evidence → Eliminate → Answer**

Do not depend on memory alone when the passage is available.
:::

# 4. Main Idea

The **main idea** is the central point the passage communicates.

It is usually broader than any single example or detail.

### Example

Suppose a passage discusses how regular exercise improves physical health, mood, sleep, and concentration.

The main idea is likely:

> **Regular exercise has several important benefits for overall well-being.**

Not:

> Exercise improves sleep.

Sleep is only one supporting detail.

::: trick
Ask: **“If I had to explain the whole passage in one sentence, what would I say?”**
:::

# 5. Finding the Main Idea

Look for:

1. Repeated concepts
2. Topic sentence
3. Supporting examples
4. Final conclusion
5. The relationship between paragraphs

::: warning
A sentence containing a number, example, person, date, or single fact is usually a **detail**, not automatically the main idea.
:::

# 6. Fact-Based Questions

A fact/detail question asks for information **directly stated or clearly presented** in the passage.

### Method

1. Identify the keyword in the question.
2. Locate the relevant part of the passage.
3. Read one or two surrounding sentences.
4. Match the option with the text.
5. Reject options that add unsupported information.

::: trap
An option can contain a true statement in real life but still be wrong if the passage does not support it when the question asks what the passage states.
:::

# 7. Inference Questions

An inference is a conclusion that can reasonably be drawn from the information provided.

It is **not** a guess or outside knowledge.

### Example

Passage says:

> The roads were flooded after continuous heavy rain, and several schools remained closed.

Reasonable inference:

> Heavy rain disrupted normal public activity.

Unreasonable inference:

> The government declared a national emergency.

The second statement is not supported.

::: strategy
For inference questions ask:

**“What must/likely follows from the evidence?”**

Do not ask, “What could possibly be true?”
:::

# 8. Vocabulary in Context

A word may have different meanings in different contexts.

## Context Method

1. Read the complete sentence.
2. Read the sentence before and after it.
3. Identify the part of speech.
4. Replace the word mentally with each option.
5. Choose the meaning that fits the passage.

### Example

> The scientist **observed** a change in the sample.

Here **observed** means **noticed/watched carefully**, not “followed a rule”.

::: tip
Never choose a dictionary meaning automatically. Choose the meaning that fits the **specific context**.
:::

# 9. Reference Questions

Reference questions ask what a pronoun or phrase refers to.

Common reference words:

- it
- they
- he/she
- this
- that
- these
- those
- such
- former/latter

### Example

> The city introduced electric buses. **This** reduced pollution.

**This** refers to the introduction/use of electric buses described in the previous sentence.

::: trick
Look **backward first**. Identify the nearest grammatically and logically suitable antecedent, then verify the meaning.
:::

# 10. Suitable Title

The best title should:

- cover the central idea
- be concise
- represent the whole passage
- avoid unnecessary detail

### Avoid

- titles that are too broad
- titles based on one small example
- titles containing information absent from the passage
- sensational or exaggerated titles

::: tip
**Best Title = Main Idea in a short form.**
:::

# 11. Cause and Effect

Look for explicit or implied relationships:

**Cause → Effect**

Common cause markers:

- because
- since
- due to
- owing to
- as

Common effect markers:

- therefore
- hence
- thus
- consequently
- as a result

### Example

> The river overflowed because of continuous rainfall.

Cause → continuous rainfall  
Effect → river overflow

# 12. Sequence and Chronology

Questions may ask what happened first, next, or last.

Look for:

- first
- initially
- then
- next
- later
- afterwards
- finally
- eventually

::: strategy
Create a simple mental timeline rather than trying to remember every sentence word-for-word.
:::

# 13. Writer's Purpose

The writer may primarily intend to:

- inform
- explain
- describe
- compare
- persuade
- warn
- criticize
- suggest

The purpose should be inferred from the **whole passage**, not one sentence.

# 14. Tone of the Passage

Tone is the writer's attitude toward the subject.

Common tones:

- neutral
- informative
- positive
- critical
- concerned
- optimistic
- pessimistic
- appreciative
- cautious

::: warning
Do not confuse **topic** with **tone**. A passage about pollution can have a neutral, concerned, critical, or solution-oriented tone depending on the writer's language.
:::

# 15. Fact vs Opinion

### Fact
A statement that can generally be verified.

### Opinion
A belief, judgment, or evaluation.

Signal words may include:

- I believe
- in my view
- should
- best
- terrible
- wonderful

::: trap
Words such as **should, best, worst, excellent, terrible** can signal evaluation or opinion, but always judge the complete sentence and context.
:::

# 16. Positive and Negative Questions

Be careful with questions containing:

- NOT
- EXCEPT
- FALSE
- INCORRECT
- CANNOT
- NOT TRUE

::: warning
Circle or mentally highlight the negative word. Many comprehension mistakes happen because the student finds a true option when the question actually asks for the **false/incorrect** one.
:::

# 17. Option Elimination

Eliminate options that are:

### 1. Contradictory
The passage says the opposite.

### 2. Unsupported
The passage gives no evidence.

### 3. Too Extreme
Uses words such as **always, never, completely, only** when the passage is qualified.

### 4. Too Narrow
Describes only one detail instead of the whole idea.

### 5. Partly Correct
Contains one correct point but adds an unsupported claim.

::: strategy
**Correct answer = supported + relevant + complete + appropriately worded.**
:::

# 18. Extreme Words

Be cautious with:

- always
- never
- all
- none
- completely
- only
- must
- impossible

These are not automatically wrong, but they require strong textual support.

# 19. Paragraph Mapping

For longer passages, mentally label paragraphs:

- **P1:** Introduction/topic
- **P2:** Explanation/evidence
- **P3:** Example/contrast
- **P4:** Conclusion/solution

This makes detail questions easier to locate.

::: trick
Do not reread the entire passage for every question. Use **keyword → paragraph → evidence**.
:::

# 20. Reading Speed vs Accuracy

Speed matters, but accuracy is more important.

### Improve speed by:

- reading groups of words rather than every word separately
- recognising connectors
- avoiding unnecessary rereading
- locating keywords quickly
- practising regularly

### Improve accuracy by:

- checking negative words
- returning to the passage
- distinguishing fact from inference
- eliminating extreme options
- verifying the exact question requirement

# 21. Difficult Vocabulary During Reading

You do not need to understand every word to understand a passage.

If a word is unfamiliar:

1. Ignore it temporarily.
2. Read the surrounding sentence.
3. Determine whether it is central to the question.
4. Infer meaning from context if necessary.
5. Return to it only if required.

::: tip
**Do not stop the entire passage because of one unknown word.** Maintain the overall meaning first.
:::

# 22. Common Comprehension Traps

::: trap
### Trap 1 — Outside Knowledge
Choosing an answer because you know it is true outside the passage.

### Trap 2 — Detail as Main Idea
Selecting a small supporting fact as the title/main idea.

### Trap 3 — Extreme Option
Choosing an option stronger than the passage.

### Trap 4 — Half-Correct Option
An option begins correctly but adds an unsupported conclusion.

### Trap 5 — Reference Error
Selecting a grammatically nearby noun that does not make logical sense.

### Trap 6 — Negative Question
Missing words such as NOT, EXCEPT, or FALSE.
:::

# 24. Solved Mini Passage 1 — Main Idea

### Passage

Regular physical activity can improve several aspects of health. It can strengthen the body, support better sleep, and improve mood. Even moderate activity, when performed regularly, can contribute to a healthier lifestyle.

### Question
What is the main idea?

A. Sleep is the only benefit of exercise.  
B. Regular physical activity has several health benefits.  
C. Only intense exercise is useful.  
D. Exercise is difficult for everyone.

**Answer: B**

**Why?** The passage discusses several benefits, not only sleep or intense exercise.

# 25. Solved Mini Passage 2 — Inference

### Passage

The village had received very little rainfall for several months. The wells were drying up, and farmers had begun using water carefully for crops.

### Question
What can reasonably be inferred?

A. The village was experiencing water scarcity.  
B. The village received record rainfall.  
C. All crops had already failed.  
D. The farmers stopped working completely.

**Answer: A**

**Reason:** Low rainfall + drying wells + careful water use indicate water scarcity.

# 26. Solved Mini Passage 3 — Vocabulary

### Passage

The teacher gave a **brief** explanation before the test began.

### Question
The word **brief** most nearly means:

A. lengthy  
B. short  
C. confusing  
D. difficult

**Answer: B**

# 27. Solved Mini Passage 4 — Reference

### Passage

**Answer: C**

# 33. Final Reading Strategy

::: remember
### Before reading
Understand the topic and question requirement.

### While reading
Follow the main idea, paragraph purpose, connectors, examples, and contrasts.

### While answering
Locate evidence, eliminate unsupported/extreme options, and distinguish fact from inference.

### Before marking
Check whether the question asks **TRUE, FALSE, NOT, EXCEPT, or INCORRECT**.
:::

# 34. Quick Revision Checklist

- [ ] Identify the main idea.
- [ ] Separate main idea from supporting details.
- [ ] Locate explicit facts.
- [ ] Make only evidence-based inferences.
- [ ] Use context for vocabulary.
- [ ] Trace pronoun/reference words.
- [ ] Select a title covering the whole passage.
- [ ] Identify cause and effect.
- [ ] Follow chronological order.
- [ ] Recognise writer's purpose and tone.
- [ ] Watch negative question words.
- [ ] Eliminate extreme/unsupported options.

::: remember
**Reading Comprehension Formula:**

**Understand the passage → Locate evidence → Eliminate traps → Select the best-supported answer.**
:::
