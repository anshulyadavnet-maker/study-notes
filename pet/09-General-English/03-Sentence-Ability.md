# 1. What is Sentence Ability?

Sentence ability is the ability to understand whether a sentence is **grammatically correct, logically complete, properly ordered, and appropriate in context**.

For PET-level questions, the most useful approach is to combine grammar knowledge with sentence meaning.

::: tip
**Exam Tip:** Do not correct a sentence only because it “sounds unusual”. Identify the exact grammatical or usage rule being violated.
:::

# 2. Sentence Structure Basics

A basic English sentence may contain:

**Subject + Verb + Object/Complement**

> **Ravi** **reads** **books**.

- **Subject:** who/what the sentence is about or who performs the action.
- **Verb:** action/state.
- **Object:** receives the action.
- **Complement:** completes the meaning of the subject/object.

## 2.1 Phrase vs Clause

**Phrase:** group of words functioning as a unit, normally without a finite verb.

> in the morning

**Clause:** group of words containing a subject–verb relationship.

> when he arrived

## 2.2 Sentence Types by Structure

### Simple Sentence
One independent clause.

> She works hard.

### Compound Sentence
Two or more independent clauses joined appropriately.

> She works hard, **and** she studies regularly.

### Complex Sentence
One main clause and one or more subordinate clauses.

> She studies hard **because she wants to succeed**.

::: concept
Understanding sentence structure makes **error detection and sentence arrangement** much easier because you can identify which words belong together.
:::

# 3. Sentence Correction

Sentence correction questions ask you to identify and fix an error or choose the best grammatical form.

## 3.1 Correction Checklist

Check the sentence in this order:

1. **Subject–Verb Agreement**
2. **Tense and verb form**
3. **Pronoun**
4. **Article / Determiner**
5. **Preposition**
6. **Adjective / Adverb**
7. **Conjunction**
8. **Word order**
9. **Parallel structure**
10. **Meaning and logical fit**

::: strategy
**Three-step method:**

**Locate → Identify rule → Correct**

Do not change more words than necessary.
:::

# 4. Subject–Verb Errors

### Rule
A singular subject generally takes a singular verb in the present simple; a plural subject generally takes a plural verb.

❌ He **go** to school.  
✅ He **goes** to school.

❌ The students **is** ready.  
✅ The students **are** ready.

### Intervening Phrase

❌ The quality of these products **are** good.  
✅ The quality of these products **is** good.

::: trap
Do not select the verb according to the nearest noun. Find the actual subject.
:::

# 5. Tense and Verb-Form Errors

### After Do/Does/Did
Use the base form (V1).

❌ Did he **went** there?  
✅ Did he **go** there?

❌ She does not **likes** tea.  
✅ She does not **like** tea.

### After Modals
Use V1.

❌ He can **swims**.  
✅ He can **swim**.

### Perfect Forms
Use **have/has/had + V3**.

❌ She has **went** home.  
✅ She has **gone** home.

::: remember
**did + V1**  
**modal + V1**  
**have/has/had + V3**
:::

# 6. Pronoun Errors

Pronouns should agree with and clearly refer to their antecedents.

❌ Riya told Priya that **she** had won.  
This may be ambiguous without context.

Better when clarity is required:

> Riya told Priya, “I have won.”

### Common Case Error

In formal standard usage:

❌ Between you and **I**  
✅ Between you and **me**

::: tip
After a preposition, use the appropriate object form: **me, him, her, us, them**.
:::

# 7. Article and Determiner Errors

### A/An
Choose by sound:

❌ an university  
✅ a university

❌ a hour  
✅ an hour

### Much/Many

- **many** + countable plural noun
- **much** + uncountable noun

❌ much students  
✅ many students

### Few/Little

- few → countable plural
- little → uncountable

::: trap
Do not confuse **a few** with **few**, or **a little** with **little**. The article changes the meaning.
:::

# 8. Preposition Errors

Common exam patterns:

- good **at** English
- interested **in** music
- depend **on** someone
- consist **of** parts
- listen **to** music
- discuss something (normally without **about**)

❌ He discussed **about** the issue.  
✅ He discussed the issue.

::: warning
Prepositions are often tested through fixed combinations. Learn them as **word + preposition** units rather than translating directly from Hindi.
:::

# 9. Adjective and Adverb Errors

### Adjective
Modifies a noun/pronoun.

> She is a **careful** driver.

### Adverb
Often modifies a verb.

> She drives **carefully**.

::: trap
After linking verbs such as **be, seem, look, feel, become** an adjective is often required when describing the subject.

> She looks **beautiful**.
:::

# 10. Comparison Errors

### Comparative

Use comparative forms for comparison between two items in the usual exam pattern.

> Ravi is **taller than** Mohan.

### Superlative

> Ravi is **the tallest** boy in the class.

Avoid double comparison:

❌ more better  
❌ most tallest  
✅ better  
✅ tallest

::: tip
Watch for **than** with comparative constructions and **the** with many superlative constructions.
:::

# 11. Conjunction Errors

Correlative conjunctions should be used in pairs:

- either...or
- neither...nor
- both...and
- not only...but also

> She is **not only intelligent but also hardworking**.

::: warning
Maintain parallel structure after paired conjunctions.
:::

# 12. Parallelism

Parallelism means using the same grammatical form for items in a series or comparison.

❌ She likes **reading, to write, and dancing**.

✅ She likes **reading, writing, and dancing**.

::: concept
When a sentence lists items, check whether the items perform the same grammatical role.
:::

# 13. Modifiers and Word Order

A modifier should be placed close enough to the word it modifies to avoid confusion.

### Misplaced Modifier

> She almost **finished all the work**.  

Changing modifier position can change the intended meaning.

### Basic Word Order

English generally follows:

**Subject → Verb → Object**

> The student **solved** the problem.

::: tip
Hindi and English may arrange information differently. In error detection, follow standard English word order rather than direct translation.
:::

# 14. Fill in the Blanks

Fill-in-the-blank questions test grammar, vocabulary, collocation, and context.

## 14.1 Step-by-Step Method

1. Read the **whole sentence**.
2. Predict what kind of word is required.
3. Check grammar.
4. Check meaning.
5. Test each option in context.
6. Read the completed sentence again.

## 14.2 Grammar Clues

### Article clue
> He is ___ honest man. → **an**

### Preposition clue
> She is good ___ English. → **at**

### Tense clue
> He ___ here since 2020. → **has lived / has been living**, depending on context.

### Agreement clue
> Everyone ___ ready. → **is**

::: strategy
Do not fill the blank as soon as you see a familiar option. Read the **complete sentence after filling it** and verify both grammar and meaning.
:::

# 15. Vocabulary-Based Fillers

Some blanks require a word whose meaning fits the context.

> The instructions were very ___, so everyone understood them.

Possible answer: **clear**.

::: tip
If two options are grammatically possible, choose the one that fits the **meaning and natural usage** of the sentence.
:::

# 16. Collocations

A collocation is a combination of words that commonly occur together.

Examples:

- **make** a decision
- **do** homework
- **take** a break
- **pay** attention
- **heavy** rain
- **strong** tea
- **deep** sleep

::: warning
Direct word-for-word translation can produce unnatural English. Learn common collocations as complete units.
:::

# 17. Error Detection

In error-detection questions, a sentence may be divided into parts.

Example:

> He / do / his work / regularly.

Error → **do**  
Correction → **does**

## Error Detection Order

**Subject–Verb → Tense → Pronoun → Article → Preposition → Modifier → Vocabulary**

::: trick
If a sentence looks long, do not read every word with equal attention. First identify its **subject, main verb, and object/complement**. Then inspect the likely error zones.
:::

# 18. Sentence Arrangement

Sentence arrangement questions require you to put parts/sentences into a logical order.

## 18.1 Opening Sentence Clues

An opening sentence often:

- introduces the topic
- names the person/thing before pronouns refer to it
- provides the general idea before details

A sentence beginning with **he, she, this, that, these, such, therefore, however** is often less likely to be the first sentence when its reference has not yet been introduced.

## 18.2 Linking Clues

Look for:

- pronoun → earlier noun
- this/that → previous idea
- therefore → preceding cause/evidence
- however → contrast with previous idea
- first → later sequence
- finally → end-stage clue

::: trick
**Noun before Pronoun** is a useful clue:

“Ravi entered the room. **He** sat down.”

The sentence introducing Ravi logically comes before the sentence using **he**.
:::

# 19. Chronological Order

When sentences describe events, identify the time sequence.

Useful markers:

**first → then → next → after that → finally**

::: strategy
For arrangement questions, first identify a likely **opening sentence**, then connect sentences using pronouns, connectors, chronology, and cause-effect relationships.
:::

# 20. Cause and Effect

Look for relationships such as:

**Cause → Result**

> It rained heavily. **Therefore**, the match was cancelled.

Common result markers:

- therefore
- hence
- thus
- consequently
- as a result

Common cause markers:

- because
- since
- as
- due to
- owing to

# 21. Contrast and Continuation

### Contrast markers

- however
- but
- although
- nevertheless
- whereas

### Addition/continuation markers

- moreover
- furthermore
- also
- in addition
- besides

::: trap
A sentence beginning with **however** usually needs a preceding idea to contrast with. Do not automatically place it first.
:::

# 22. Sentence Arrangement Mini Example

Arrange:

**A. He opened the book.**  
**B. Ravi sat at his desk.**  
**C. He began to read.**

Logical order: **B → A → C**

Reason:

1. Ravi is introduced.
2. **He** refers to Ravi.
3. Reading follows opening the book.

# 23. Common Sentence Ability Traps

::: trap
### Trap 1 — Nearest noun
The nearest noun is not necessarily the subject.

### Trap 2 — Direct translation
Hindi sentence order does not always map directly to English.

### Trap 3 — Familiar option
A familiar word may still be grammatically or contextually wrong.

### Trap 4 — Connector without reference
Words such as however, therefore, this, that, and such often depend on earlier context.

### Trap 5 — Pronoun before antecedent
A pronoun may be grammatically possible but logically unclear if its reference has not been established.
:::
