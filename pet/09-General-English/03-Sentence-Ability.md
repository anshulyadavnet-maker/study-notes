# 1. What is Sentence Ability?

Sentence ability means understanding whether a sentence is **grammatically correct, logically complete, properly ordered, and appropriate in context**.

For PET, sentence ability is built from grammar, vocabulary, collocation, context, and logical sequencing.

::: tip
**Exam Tip:** Never change a sentence simply because it sounds unusual. Identify the exact grammar, usage, or logic rule first.
:::

# 2. Sentence Structure Basics

A basic sentence can be represented as:

**Subject + Verb + Object / Complement**

> Ravi reads books.

- **Subject:** the person/thing about which the sentence is made.
- **Verb:** action, occurrence, or state.
- **Object:** receives the action.
- **Complement:** completes the meaning of the subject/object.

## 2.1 Phrase vs Clause

**Phrase:** a group of words functioning as a unit, normally without a finite verb.

> in the morning

**Clause:** a group of words containing a subject–verb relationship.

> when he arrived

## 2.2 Sentence Types by Structure

### Simple Sentence
One independent clause.

> She works hard.

### Compound Sentence
Two or more independent clauses joined appropriately.

> She works hard, and she studies regularly.

### Complex Sentence
One independent clause and one or more subordinate clauses.

> She studies hard because she wants to succeed.

::: concept
Sentence structure is the foundation of **correction, improvement, error detection, and arrangement** questions.
:::

# 3. Sentence Correction

Sentence correction asks you to identify an incorrect form and replace it with the grammatically or contextually correct form.

## 3.1 Correction Checklist

Check in this order:

1. Subject–Verb Agreement
2. Tense and verb form
3. Pronoun
4. Article / Determiner
5. Preposition
6. Adjective / Adverb
7. Conjunction
8. Word order
9. Parallel structure
10. Meaning and logical fit
11. Collocation / idiomatic usage

::: strategy
**Locate → Identify the rule → Correct → Re-read the complete sentence.**

Change only what is necessary.
:::

# 4. Subject–Verb Errors

A singular subject generally takes a singular verb in the present simple; a plural subject generally takes a plural verb.

❌ He **go** to school.  
✅ He **goes** to school.

❌ The students **is** ready.  
✅ The students **are** ready.

## Intervening Phrases

❌ The quality of these products **are** good.  
✅ The quality of these products **is** good.

The subject is **quality**, not products.

::: trap
Do not select the verb according to the nearest noun. Find the grammatical subject first.
:::

# 5. Tense and Verb-Form Errors

## After Do / Does / Did
Use V1.

❌ Did he **went** there?  
✅ Did he **go** there?

❌ She does not **likes** tea.  
✅ She does not **like** tea.

## After Modals
Use V1.

❌ He can **swims**.  
✅ He can **swim**.

## Perfect Forms
Use **have/has/had + V3**.

❌ She has **went** home.  
✅ She has **gone** home.

::: remember
**did + V1**  
**modal + V1**  
**have/has/had + V3**
:::

# 6. Pronoun Errors

A pronoun should clearly and appropriately refer to its antecedent.

❌ Riya told Priya that **she** had won.  
This may be ambiguous without context.

### Pronoun Case

After a preposition, use the appropriate object form in formal standard usage:

❌ Between you and **I**  
✅ Between you and **me**

::: tip
Always identify the antecedent before selecting a pronoun.
:::

# 7. Article and Determiner Errors

## A / An
Choose according to sound.

❌ an university  
✅ a university

❌ a hour  
✅ an hour

## Much / Many

- **many** + countable plural noun
- **much** + uncountable noun

❌ much students  
✅ many students

## Few / A Few / Little / A Little

- few → almost none; countable plural
- a few → some; countable plural
- little → almost none; uncountable
- a little → some; uncountable

::: trap
The article can change the meaning: **few** and **a few** are not equivalent.
:::

# 8. Preposition Errors

Common combinations:

- good **at** English
- interested **in** music
- depend **on** someone
- consist **of** parts
- listen **to** music
- responsible **for** the work
- afraid **of** dogs
- different **from** another

❌ He discussed **about** the issue.  
✅ He discussed the issue.

::: warning
Learn prepositions with the word they normally accompany. Direct translation from Hindi often creates errors.
:::

# 9. Adjective and Adverb Errors

**Adjective:** modifies a noun/pronoun.

> She is a **careful** driver.

**Adverb:** modifies a verb, adjective, adverb, or clause.

> She drives **carefully**.

After linking verbs such as **be, seem, look, feel, become**, an adjective is often used to describe the subject.

> She looks **beautiful**.

::: trap
Do not automatically add **-ly** to make an adverb. Word function and meaning matter.
:::

# 10. Comparison Errors

### Comparative

> Ravi is **taller than** Mohan.

### Superlative

> Ravi is **the tallest** boy in the class.

Avoid double comparison:

❌ more better  
❌ most tallest  
✅ better  
✅ tallest

Common irregular forms:

- good → better → best
- bad → worse → worst
- little → less → least
- many/much → more → most

# 11. Conjunction Errors

Common correlative pairs:

- either...or
- neither...nor
- both...and
- not only...but also
- whether...or

> She is **not only intelligent but also hardworking**.

::: warning
Maintain parallel grammatical structures after paired conjunctions.
:::

# 12. Parallelism

Parallelism means using the same grammatical form for items in a series or comparison.

❌ She likes **reading, to write, and dancing**.

✅ She likes **reading, writing, and dancing**.

Other examples:

❌ He wanted to **study, working, and to travel**.  
✅ He wanted to **study, work, and travel**.

::: concept
When several items are joined by **and/or**, check whether they perform the same grammatical role.
:::

# 13. Modifiers and Word Order

A modifier should be positioned so that its intended meaning is clear.

### Basic Word Order

**Subject → Verb → Object**

> The student solved the problem.

### Misplaced Modifier

> She almost finished all the work.

The position of **almost** affects what is being limited.

::: tip
English generally depends more strongly on word order than Hindi. Avoid direct word-for-word translation.
:::

# 14. Sentence Improvement

Sentence improvement questions present a sentence with an underlined or highlighted part. You must select the option that gives the **best grammatical and contextual form**.

## 14.1 Improvement Strategy

1. Read the complete sentence.
2. Identify the faulty portion.
3. Determine the rule involved.
4. Check whether the original may already be correct.
5. Compare the alternatives.
6. Choose the most natural and precise form.

::: strategy
**Do not improve only grammar.** Check grammar + meaning + tense + collocation + logical consistency.
:::

## 14.2 Common Improvement Areas

- subject–verb agreement
- tense
- articles
- prepositions
- pronouns
- comparison
- parallelism
- word choice
- collocation
- redundancy

### Examples

**1.** He is senior **than** me.  
→ He is senior **to** me.

**2.** She returned **back** home.  
→ She returned home.

**3.** He discussed **about** the issue.  
→ He discussed the issue.

**4.** She is **more better** than her sister.  
→ She is better than her sister.

::: warning
Some improvement questions contain an option such as **“No improvement”**. Do not change a correct sentence unnecessarily.
:::

# 15. Redundancy

Redundancy occurs when unnecessary words repeat the same meaning.

Common examples:

- return back → return
- repeat again → repeat
- discuss about → discuss
- enter into the room → enter the room (when “enter” means go into)
- revert back → revert

::: trap
Not every repeated-looking phrase is automatically wrong; judge the actual construction and meaning.
:::

# 16. Fill in the Blanks

Fill-in-the-blank questions can test grammar, vocabulary, collocation, and context.

## Step-by-Step Method

1. Read the entire sentence.
2. Predict the required part of speech.
3. Check grammar.
4. Check meaning.
5. Check collocation.
6. Insert the best option.
7. Re-read the full sentence.

### Grammar Clues

> He is ___ honest man. → **an**

> She is good ___ English. → **at**

> Everyone ___ ready. → **is**

> She has ___ the work. → **completed**

### Context Clue

> The instructions were very ___, so everyone understood them.

The answer should express **clarity**, not simply fit grammatically.

# 17. Cloze-Style Context Fillers

A cloze passage contains several blanks. Each blank must be solved using the **local grammar and the overall meaning of the passage**.

## Cloze Strategy

1. Read the complete passage once.
2. Identify the topic and tone.
3. Predict the missing word.
4. Check grammar around the blank.
5. Check the meaning of the whole sentence.
6. Re-read the passage after filling all blanks.

::: strategy
Do not solve every cloze blank independently. Later sentences can provide clues for earlier blanks.
:::

# 18. Collocations

A collocation is a combination of words that commonly occur together.

Examples:

- **make** a decision
- **do** homework
- **take** a break
- **pay** attention
- **catch** a bus
- **heavy** rain
- **strong** tea
- **deep** sleep
- **commit** a crime
- **keep** a promise

::: warning
A grammatically possible word is not always the natural word. Collocation can determine the correct answer.
:::

# 19. Error Detection

In error-detection questions, a sentence may be divided into parts.

> He / do / his work / regularly.

Error → **do**  
Correction → **does**

## Error Detection Order

**Subject–Verb → Tense → Pronoun → Article/Determiner → Preposition → Modifier → Parallelism → Vocabulary/Collocation**

::: trick
For a long sentence, first locate the **main subject + main verb + object/complement**. Then inspect the likely error zones.
:::

# 20. Mixed Grammar Errors

A sentence may contain more than one possible-looking problem. Identify the error that actually violates standard grammar or the intended meaning.

Example:

> The students of this class **is** preparing for the test.

Main error → **is**  
Correction → **are**

The phrase **of this class** does not change the plural subject **students**.

# 21. Sentence Arrangement

Sentence arrangement requires you to put sentences or parts into a logical sequence.

## 21.1 Opening Sentence

A likely opening sentence often:

- introduces the topic
- introduces the person/thing
- gives the general background
- does not depend on an earlier reference

A sentence beginning with **he, she, this, that, these, such, therefore** is often unlikely to be the first sentence if its reference has not yet been introduced.

## 21.2 Pronoun Link

> Ravi entered the room. **He** sat down.

The sentence introducing Ravi normally precedes the sentence beginning with **he**.

## 21.3 Demonstrative Link

Words such as **this, that, these, those, such** often refer to an earlier idea or noun.

## 21.4 Connector Link

- however → contrast
- therefore → result
- moreover → addition
- consequently → result
- finally → concluding stage

::: trick
Look for **mandatory pairs** before trying every possible order. If one sentence clearly explains “this”, find the sentence that introduces the relevant idea.
:::

# 22. Chronological Order

When events occur over time, use the sequence:

**first → then → next → after that → finally**

Other clues include dates, ages, stages, and time expressions.

# 23. Cause and Effect

Common pattern:

**Cause → Result**

> It rained heavily. **Therefore**, the match was cancelled.

### Result markers

therefore, hence, thus, consequently, as a result

### Cause markers

because, since, as, due to, owing to

::: tip
A result marker normally connects an effect to an earlier cause or explanation.
:::

# 24. Contrast and Continuation

### Contrast

however, but, although, nevertheless, whereas, yet

### Addition

moreover, furthermore, also, in addition, besides

::: trap
**However** normally signals a contrast with a preceding idea. Do not automatically make it the first sentence of a paragraph.
:::

# 25. Advanced Sentence Arrangement Technique

Use a **link-chain** approach:

**Opening → Noun introduction → Pronoun/reference → Explanation → Result/example → Conclusion**

### Strong clues

1. Full name → pronoun
2. General statement → specific example
3. Cause → effect
4. Question → answer/explanation
5. Problem → solution
6. First event → later event
7. Claim → evidence

::: strategy
If options are given as sequences, find a strong pair first. Once one pair is fixed, eliminate sequences that break that pair.
:::

# 26. Question Formation

Correct question structure is useful in sentence correction and transformation.

## Yes/No Questions

**Auxiliary + Subject + Main Verb...?**

> Does he work here?

## WH Questions

**WH-word + Auxiliary + Subject + Main Verb...?**

> Where does he live?

::: trap
After an auxiliary such as **do/does/did**, use V1:

❌ Where did he went?  
✅ Where did he go?
:::

# 27. Negative Sentence Formation

Common patterns:

- Present simple → do/does not + V1
- Past simple → did not + V1
- Be verb → be + not
- Modal → modal + not + V1
- Perfect → have/has/had + not + V3

Examples:

> He does not work here.  
> She did not go.  
> He cannot swim.  
> They have not finished.

# 28. Punctuation Basics for Sentence Ability

Basic punctuation can affect sentence clarity.

### Full Stop
Ends a statement.

### Comma
Separates items, introductory elements, or clauses in appropriate constructions.

### Apostrophe
Shows possession or contraction.

- Ravi's book
- don't = do not

### Question Mark
Used after a direct question.

> Where are you going?

### Quotation Marks
Used for direct speech in standard English writing.

::: warning
Do not confuse an apostrophe for possession with a plural marker: **book's** does not normally mean “books”.
:::

# 29. Common Sentence Ability Traps

::: trap
### Trap 1 — Nearest noun
The nearest noun is not necessarily the subject.

### Trap 2 — Direct translation
Hindi sentence order does not always map directly to English.

### Trap 3 — Familiar option
A familiar word may still be grammatically or contextually wrong.

### Trap 4 — Connector without reference
However, therefore, this, that, and such often depend on earlier context.

### Trap 5 — Pronoun before antecedent
A pronoun may be unclear when its reference has not been established.

### Trap 6 — Double comparison
more better / most tallest.

### Trap 7 — Redundancy
return back / repeat again / discuss about.

### Trap 8 — Collocation
A word can be grammatically possible but unnatural with the surrounding words.

### Trap 9 — No improvement
Do not alter a sentence merely to make it different.
:::
